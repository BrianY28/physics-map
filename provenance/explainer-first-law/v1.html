Newton's first law: inertia

A puck on a frictionless track never stops. A ball on the floor of a braking bus slides forward. One rule covers both: a body keeps its velocity until a net force changes it.

Open this node in the mind map ↗

§ 1 The law itself

In one plain sentence: a body keeps moving in a straight line at a constant speed, or stays at rest, unless a net force acts on it.

"Uniform motion" means constant velocity. The speed stays the same and the direction stays the same. A body moving uniformly at 5 m/s east is at 5 m/s east a second later, and a minute later, and a century later, as long as nothing pushes or pulls it.

Rest is the special case of zero velocity. The law treats rest and steady straight-line motion as the same physical situation, which is the surprise Newton inherited from Galileo and Descartes: there is no absolute distinction between the two.

Newton's Latin is careful about the qualifier. Nisi quatenus a viribus impressis cogitur means "unless, and to the extent that, it is compelled by impressed forces". Any nonzero net force changes the velocity. A zero net force leaves it alone.

§ 2 Friction dial

A puck slides along a level track at 6.00 m/s. Kinetic friction gives it a deceleration a = μg, so the net force per kilogram is μg as well, with g = 9.81 m/s². Pull μ down to zero and the puck stops slowing. Watch the speed–time graph flatten into a horizontal line.

§ 3 Galileo's two ramps

Release a ball from height h on one inclined plane and let it climb a second plane. With no friction it always returns to the same height h. Make the second plane shallower and the ball must travel farther to get there, because the distance up the plane is d = h / sin θ. Flatten the second plane completely and the ball never reaches h again: it rolls on forever at constant speed.

Galileo built this argument from his inclined-plane experiments, published in the Two New Sciences (1638), and made the broader case for persisting horizontal motion in the Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems (1632).

§ 4 Which frame?

A bus brakes at 5.0 m/s² from 4.0 m/s. A ball rests on its floor, and the floor is frictionless, so the ball feels no horizontal force at all. Watch the same event from the street and from a seat in the bus.

Ground frame: the ball keeps a constant velocity of 4.0 m/s while the bus slows. Net force on the ball is zero, so its motion stays uniform. The first law holds here.

In the street frame, the ball has zero net force and zero acceleration, exactly as the first law says. In the bus frame the ball accelerates forward at 5.0 m/s² while nothing pushes it. The first law fails there.

This is why the first law says something the second law cannot. F = ma is a working equation only inside frames where a body with no net force has no acceleration. The first law asserts that such frames exist and tells you how to recognise one. Inside the bus you must bolt on a fictitious force, −ma, before F = ma gives the right answer again. The law's real job is to define the stage on which the rest of mechanics runs.

§ 5 Three misconceptions

Claim

"A moving object needs a force to keep it moving."

Correction

This is Aristotle's view, and it matches everyday life because friction and air drag act on everything around us. The push you give a box is the force that cancels friction, so the net force is close to zero and the box moves at a steady speed. Take friction away and the box keeps its velocity with no push at all. Plate I shows the two cases side by side: set μ above zero and the puck stops, set μ to zero and it never does.

Claim

"Objects naturally slow down."

Correction

Nothing slows down on its own. Slowing is always the work of a force pointing against the motion. A puck on a level track with μ = 0.10 decelerates at 0.98 m/s², and that number comes entirely from friction. Set μ to 0.00 and the deceleration becomes 0.00 m/s². The speed–time graph is a straight line in both cases, with a slope that is exactly −μg.

Claim

"The first law is just F = ma with F = 0."

Correction

Putting F = 0 into F = ma gives a = 0, which is only useful once you know which frames the equation is valid in. The first law claims that frames with that property exist and identifies them by experiment. In an accelerating frame a body with F = 0 still has a ≠ 0, so the plain form of the second law breaks down there. Newton's laws define inertial frames and then reduce mechanics to solving differential equations inside them, and the definition is the first law's contribution.

§ 6 How the idea arrived

  1. 4th century BCE

    Aristotle held that motion needs a mover. Heavy bodies fall toward their natural place, light bodies rise, and violent motion such as a thrown stone needs a continuous push. His account of projectiles had the air itself keeping the stone moving after it left the hand.

  2. 6th century CE

    John Philoponus, working in Alexandria, attacked that account of projectiles. He argued that the thrower gives the body an impressed motive force which carries it along, and that the air chiefly resists. This is the first clear statement of what later became impetus theory.

  3. 14th century

    Jean Buridan at Paris turned impetus into a quantitative theory. Impetus is proportional to weight and speed, it persists in the body, and it is worn down by resistance. Buridan noted that with no resistance an impetus would carry a body indefinitely.

  4. 1632 and 1638

    Galileo Galilei reached inertia through experiment. A ball rolling down one inclined plane and up another returns to the same height, and a shallower second plane makes it travel farther. His inertia was horizontal motion: motion that never rises or falls, which for Galileo meant a path level with the Earth's surface, so his bodies curved around the globe rather than flying off along a tangent.

  5. 1644

    René Descartes, in the Principles of Philosophy, was the first to state inertia along a straight line. Each part of matter keeps its state of motion, and that motion is rectilinear. The straight line is the crucial break from Galileo's horizontal circle.

  6. 1687

    Isaac Newton made straight-line inertia the first of the three laws in the Principia, and with the second and third laws turned it into a working system for solving motion.

§ 7 Where you meet it

  • Seat belts

    A car braking hard slows the seats, the frame and the belt anchors, but nothing slows your body until the belt pushes on it. The belt supplies the force that changes your velocity. Without it, you would carry on at the car's previous speed until something else, such as the dashboard, did the same job less gently.

  • The tablecloth trick

    Pull a cloth out from under a plate quickly and the plate stays put. Friction between cloth and plate is the only horizontal force on the plate, and it acts for such a short time that the plate's velocity barely changes. Pull slowly and the same friction acts for longer, and the plate goes with the cloth.

  • Voyager 1 coasting

    Launched in 1977, Voyager 1 leaves the solar system at roughly 17 km/s relative to the Sun, and its engines have been off for decades. Out there the net force is tiny, dominated by the Sun's gravity, so the spacecraft coasts on nearly a straight line at nearly constant speed. That is Newton's first law running for half a century.
